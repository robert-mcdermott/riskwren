The program handbook

For a risk and compliance team, most often the security office, running its program in RiskWren: what to decide first, what to do in the first months, and the rhythm of a year. The other guides say how each part works; this one says when to use it, and why.

Decide first#

A few decisions shape everything after them. Make them before you enter much, and write them down: each one is a setting you can change later, but records made under one setting are not remade under the next.

DecisionWhere it is setA good start
Who runs the programRoles: managers run it, administrators keep the installationTwo managers from the security or risk office, and an administrator who is not one of them
What the first year coversYour plan, not a settingThe systems and processes that hold restricted information, and the frameworks you are already held to
The program to start fromriskwren init --programThe one nearest your organization; adjust it under Admin → Program
The unitsAdmin → UnitsThe units that answer for things, as the organization chart has them, two or three levels deep. Not every team
Who sees the registerAdmin → Program → Who sees whatEveryone sees the inventory; the register of risks as the program decides
AppetiteAdmin → Program → Categories and appetiteAgreed with leadership, one rating for each family of risk
Who accepts whatThe program pack's authority tableUnit leaders up to moderate, division leaders up to high, the head of the organization above that
Your own wordsAdmin → Appearance → Your own wordsOnly where the standard word would confuse people

Keep the first version of the program small. A scale with five levels that everyone understands beats a precise one that nobody applies the same way. Preview every change to the program before publishing it: Admin → Program shows every risk the change would move.

The first month: the record#

  1. Units and people. Import them from a spreadsheet (Import units and people), or connect single sign-on and let roles come from the identity provider (Single sign-on). Name a steward for each unit in the first year's scope.
  2. The inventory. Start with what matters most: the critical processes and the systems they rely on, then the third parties and information assets behind them. Link them as you go: relies on, holds, shared with. A record without links is a list; with them it is a picture (Connections).
  3. Controls. Take the common controls from the library and write what each does here; add your own. Name an owner for each. Map them to your frameworks: the library's controls arrive mapped (Map controls to requirements).
  4. Scopes. One scope for each boundary you answer for: the clinical systems, a data enclave, the whole organization. Say which controls each uses and how (Scopes).
  5. Risks. Start from the library and the risk analyses rather than a blank page (Risk analyses). Rate each with reasons, inherent first; let the controls suggest the residual (What the controls suggest).

At the end of the month, readiness on the compliance dashboard is honest and mostly planned or in place. That is the right place to start.

The first quarter: the evidence#

  1. Test what matters. An assessment of each scope's key controls, results recorded by someone who does not own the control and confirmed by a second person (Assessments and tests). Readiness moves to verified only here.
  2. Collect evidence once. Attach each piece to everything it supports, with the date it holds to. It stops counting on its own (Evidence stops counting).
  3. Put failures right. A failed test opens an issue; an issue has a date from its severity, actions with owners, and is closed by someone who did not fix it (Issues and exceptions). What cannot be fixed yet is an exception, decided and dated.
  4. Policies. Load the documents you have, link each to the controls it directs and the requirements it answers, and ask the people each applies to to acknowledge it (Policies).
  5. Suppliers. Tier the third parties that hold restricted information, send each its questionnaire, and read their reports for what they cover (Third parties).
  6. Incidents. Open reporting to everyone, and decide whether to open reporting without a name (Incidents).

The yearly rhythm#

Once the record is in place, the program is the same work on a calendar. Scheduled work reminds owners before things are due, then their stewards and leaders when they are late (Reminders), so the team chases less and reads more.

WhenWhatWhere
Every weekTriage new incidents and concerns; read what is waiting on homeIncidents; Home
Every monthIssues past their date, exceptions ending, indicators past their limit, evidence about to stop countingThe compliance and risk dashboards; Indicators
Every quarterThe risk committee meets on an agenda drawn from the register; a leadership report is issued and kept as it was given; control owners attestCommittee sessions; Leadership reports; Campaigns
Twice a yearKey controls tested again; risks rated high and critical reviewedAssessments; the review cadence
Every yearAppetite agreed with leadership; the program reviewed and published; each policy reviewed; risk owners asked to look again; suppliers reviewed on their tier's cadence; internal audit's plan approved; business impact analysed again for critical processes and continuity plans exercised; privacy impact assessments repeated where the program saysAdmin → Program; Policies; Campaigns; Third parties; Audit; Reports → Resilience and Continuity plans
When something changesA new system or supplier tiered and linked; a framework's new version loaded and a scope moved to it when ready; a risk looked at again when its picture changesThe inventory; Frameworks; the register

The cadences behind this (how often each rating is reviewed, how long an exception may last, how often a key control is tested, when a plan is exercised) are the program's, under Admin → Program → Cadences.

Who does what#

RoleIn the program
Security leader, risk manager (managers)Shape the program, run campaigns and committee sessions, issue leadership reports, decide exceptions within their authority
StewardsKeep their unit's records current: inventory, risks, controls, owners
Owners of risks, controls, and actionsAnswer for one thing each, and are reminded when it is due
AssessorsTest controls, review results, confirm closures, triage incidents
LeadersAccept risks within their authority, and read their units' standing
AuditorsRead everything and change nothing; internal audit plans and works on the same record
AdministratorsAccounts, units, the look, single sign-on, tokens, and the installation
EveryoneReads the policies that apply to them, reports what goes wrong, and answers what they are asked

What to watch#

Measures say what they count and open the list behind each figure. Early on, watch coverage: how much of the inventory has owners, risks rated, and controls mapped. Later, watch movement: risks above appetite, issues past their date, controls failing a test, and readiness by scope against each framework. A figure that never moves is a figure nobody is acting on.
